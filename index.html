Capstone Project

Detecting fake hotel reviews with NLP and a booking check

We trained a classifier to tell genuine hotel reviews from deceptive ones, and put a booking check in front of it so only reviews from verified reservations reach the model. It started as a capstone project in a group of three and was later published at IEEE ICECCT 2021.

88.1%

Why fake reviews are worth solving

Reviews steer what people buy, and shoppers know they can't fully trust them. That gap is what we wanted to work on.

What shoppers told us

At the start of the project we ran a short survey. 74% of respondents said they were likely to decide based on reviews (4 or 5 on a 5-point scale). Yet 61% had felt some positive reviews were posted on the company's behalf, and only 4% said no. 51% had come across reviews written out of spite.

On 1-to-5 scales the picture is similar. Most people lean on reviews, 45% often see reviews that talk about the brand more than the product, and only 40% were likely to answer a "was this review useful?" prompt.

Who answered

156 people answered a Google Form that we shared with classmates and friends over five days in September 2020. Respondents came from every age band, from under 15 to over 45, though most (about 86%) were 16 to 25. Read it as a snapshot of young online shoppers, not a poll of everyone. Two people left age blank and we counted them as 16 to 25, as in our report.

What already existed

We surveyed the market for tools that do something similar. ReviewMeta (archived version) re-scored Amazon reviews and gave an adjusted rating. Fakespot (archived version) graded reviews on a handful of shopping sites. The Review Index (archived version) summarised the reviews for a product.

They shared two limits. Each worked only on specific sites, and each judged reviews after they were already posted, so nothing stopped the fake ones from going up. The market has moved since: Mozilla shut Fakespot down in July 2025, and ReviewMeta now appears to be offline.

What we tried instead

We combined two signals: what a review says, and who wrote it. A classifier reads the text, and a booking check confirms the reviewer actually has a reservation before the review is classified.

We trained and tested on hotel reviews because that's where a labelled dataset existed, and our time was limited. The original goal was reviews for any kind of e-commerce product, and the booking check is the hotel-specific stand-in for something like a verified purchase. Testing other product categories is the obvious next step.

The data we trained on

We didn't collect our own reviews. We used a public research dataset of hotel reviews where every review already carries a label.

What's in it

The corpus holds 1,600 reviews of 20 Chicago hotels, perfectly balanced: 800 truthful and 800 deceptive, half positive and half negative, with 80 reviews per hotel. Credit for collecting it is in the footer.

A caveat about the labels

The deceptive reviews weren't caught in the wild. They were written to order. That makes the labels reliable, but it narrows what a good score means: our model learns to tell these paid-for reviews from real ones, not to spot every fake on the internet. The truthful reviews are also assumed to be genuine.

What simple counts can't tell you

Before any NLP, we checked whether crude measurements would give fakes away: length, word and sentence counts, and the number of digits. Mostly they didn't. Characters, words and non-word characters barely correlate with the label (between −0.03 and −0.05) and are almost identical to each other. Polarity has no correlation at all (0.00), as expected from the balanced design.

The one real signal was digits: 66% of truthful reviews contain at least one, against 36% of deceptive ones (r = −0.31). Real guests may mention prices and room numbers, though we can't say that from the data alone. It was still nowhere near enough, which is why we moved on to the words themselves.

From raw text to numbers

A classifier can't read English. Most of our work went into turning a review into numbers without throwing away what makes it fake.

The shape of it

The flow is the usual one for a text classifier. Reviews are cleaned and converted to features, split into training and testing data, and used to fit five different models. Their predictions on the held-back reviews are scored, and the scores send us back to preprocessing to try again.

What we compared, and what won

Five models and six ways of counting words: 30 combinations, each scored on the same held-back reviews.

First attempt: raw text

We started by giving unprocessed review text to five classifiers: Random Forest, Multinomial Naive Bayes, a Support Vector Classifier, XGBoost and XGBoost's random-forest mode. Random Forest led on accuracy, recall and F1, and the Support Vector Classifier led on precision.

The scores looked good, but as our report puts it, the models failed to perform on unseen data that wasn't part of the dataset. The report doesn't put a number on that failure. We went back to the text, added the preprocessing from the last section, and tried n-grams.

    About the AUC column: in the notebooks we kept, AUC was computed from each model's predicted labels, not its probabilities. For a two-class problem that makes it the same as balanced accuracy, so it is a useful cross-check but not a separate measure of how well the model ranks reviews.

    How much to read into 88.1%

    Every accuracy above is a multiple of 1/320, so each score comes from 320 test reviews. At that size, one review is 0.3 points, and a rough 95% margin on 88% accuracy is about ±3.6 points. So Naive Bayes, Random Forest and the Support Vector Classifier are closer than their ranking suggests.

    The gains from preprocessing were also small and uneven. Naive Bayes improved from 85.9% on raw text to 88.1%, while Random Forest finished exactly where it started at 86.25%. The best score beats the raw-text Random Forest by 1.9 points, which is inside that margin. We'd call the result a solid baseline, not proof that one model is better.

    Checking who wrote the review

    Most fake-review work looks only at the text. We also wanted to ask whether the reviewer was ever there.

    The idea

    Before a review reaches the model, the web app asks for a username, a hotel name and a booking confirmation code, and checks them against a booking record. If they don't match, the review is turned away and never classified. Only reviews from verified reservations get a verdict.

    How it works

    The checks run in order and stop at the first failure: does the username exist, does that user have a booking at this hotel, and does the confirmation code match that booking? If all three pass, the review goes through the same cleaning and vectorizing as in training and into the classifier. The saved model and vectorizer are loaded into a small Flask app, and the result is printed as one line: genuine or fake.

    The booking record is a small table of 50 made-up bookings that we wrote for the hotels in the dataset. A real platform would ask its own booking system instead. We had no such data, so this stands in for it.

    The app itself

    The form takes four inputs, and the app answers with a verdict or one of three error messages. Pick a scenario in the replay below to see each one. It only ever ran on our own machines, so this replay is the closest you can get to it.

    What we'd flag

    The gate is blunt: it turns away anything it can't verify, including honest reviews from people who booked somewhere else, and it is only as good as the booking data behind it. It is also hotel-specific. For other products the stand-in would be something like a verified purchase.

    The two halves were also never tested together. The classifier was scored on a research corpus of reviews, and the booking table was invented to match it, so we can't say how they would behave on real traffic.

    The paper is called A Review-and-Reviewer based approach for Fake Review Detection. We presented it at the Fourth International Conference on Electrical, Computer and Communication Technologies, and it was published in the IEEE proceedings.

    Check it out on IEEE Xplore: doi: 10.1109/ICECCT52121.2021.9616697

    What it says

    The argument is simple. Most earlier work judges either the text of a review or the person who wrote it, and we tried both for hotel reviews. The paper walks through the NLP pipeline (tokenizing, lemmatizing, vectorizing), compares five classifiers on the same features, and puts the best one behind a web app that also checks the reviewer's details. Multinomial Naive Bayes on single words and pairs came out on top at 88.1% accuracy.

    What it leaves open

    The paper is upfront about two things. Asking users to type in their own details is tedious, so it suggests building that information into the model itself. And it says the approach could carry over to other services such as hospitals, restaurants, theatres and shops. We never tested either idea.

    From project to pitch

    The same project was reused for a course on technology entrepreneurship, where we played a startup selling it.

    The exercise

    The course asked us to act as a company, with the output of our project work as our product. Over its assignments we made a brochure, a pamphlet, an advertisement video and a website.

    The pitch

    The pitch was a service for e-commerce platforms that filters fraudulent reviews as they are posted, so shoppers never see them. What we said set it apart was that it uses several signals together, the review text and the reviewer, rather than judging text alone.

    Visit the startup's website, built for the same exercise, hosted on Google Sites.

    Where the pitch ran ahead of the project

    Pitches reward confidence, and ours got ahead of what we had shown. We pitched e-commerce in general, but our model had only ever seen hotel reviews. The exercise was useful for exactly that reason: it made the gap between the claim and the evidence easy to see.
