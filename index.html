Capstone Project

Detecting Fake Hotel Reviews with NLP and a Booking Check

We trained a classifier to tell genuine hotel reviews from deceptive ones, and put a booking check in front of it so only reviews from verified reservations reach the model. It started as a group capstone project and was later published at IEEE ICECCT.

Best test accuracy
88.1%
Labelled reviews
~ 1,600
Published

Why Fake Reviews are worth solving

Reviews steer what people buy, and shoppers know they can't fully trust them. That gap is what we wanted to work on.

What shoppers told us

At the start of the project we ran a short survey. 74% of respondents said they were likely to decide based on reviews (4 or 5 on a 5-point scale). Yet 61% had felt some positive reviews were posted on the company's behalf, and only 4% said no. 51% had come across reviews written out of spite.

What already existed

We surveyed the market for tools that do something similar. ReviewMeta (archived version) re-scored Amazon reviews and gave an adjusted rating. Fakespot (archived version) graded reviews on a handful of shopping sites. The Review Index (archived version) summarised the reviews for a product. They shared two limits. Each worked only on specific sites, and each judged reviews after they were already posted, so nothing stopped the fake ones from going up.

What we tried instead

We combined two signals: what a review says, and who wrote it. A classifier reads the text, and a booking check confirms the reviewer actually has a reservation before the review is classified. We trained and tested on an available labelled dataset of hotel reviews. The original goal was reviews for any kind of e-commerce product, and the booking check is the hotel-specific stand-in for something like a verified purchase. Testing other product categories is the obvious next step.

The data we trained on

We used a public research dataset of hotel reviews where every review already carries a label.

What's in it

The corpus holds 1,600 reviews of 20 Chicago hotels, perfectly balanced: 800 truthful and 800 deceptive, half positive and half negative, with 80 reviews per hotel.

How the 1,600 reviews were collected
Label Tone Where it came from Reviews
Truthful Positive TripAdvisor 400
Truthful Negative Mixed web sources 400
Deceptive Positive Amazon Mechanical Turk 400
Deceptive Negative Amazon Mechanical Turk 400

A caveat about the labels

The deceptive reviews weren't caught in the wild. They were written to order. That makes the labels reliable, but it narrows what a good score means: our model learns to tell these paid-for reviews from real ones, not to spot every fake on the internet. The truthful reviews are also assumed to be genuine.

What simple counts can't tell you

Before any NLP, we checked whether crude measurements would give fakes away: length, word and sentence counts, and the number of digits. Mostly they didn't. Characters, words and non-word characters barely correlate with the label (between −0.03 and −0.05) and are almost identical to each other. Polarity has no correlation at all (0.00), as expected from the balanced design.

The one real signal was digits: 66% of truthful reviews contain at least one, against 36% of deceptive ones (r = −0.31). Real guests may mention prices and room numbers, though we can't say that from the data alone. It was still nowhere near enough, which is why we moved on to the words themselves.

From raw text to numbers

A classifier can't read English. Most of our work went into turning a review into numbers without throwing away what makes it fake.

The shape of it

The flow is the usual one for a text classifier. Reviews are cleaned and converted to features, split into training and testing data, and used to fit five different models. Their predictions on the held-back reviews are scored, and the scores are sent back to preprocessing to try again.

Following one review through

    Regular expressions strip punctuation, drop single letters like “I” and “a”, and lowercase everything: 97 words left. They also do some collateral damage. “weren't” became “weren”, because the apostrophe split the word and the lone “t” was dropped.

  3. 3. Reduce words to their base form

    A WordNet lemmatizer maps each word to a dictionary form. Six words changed (highlighted). It runs without part-of-speech tags, so it treats every word as a noun: “towels” correctly becomes “towel”, but “was” becomes “wa” and “has” becomes “ha”. The model only needs consistency, so it's harmless, but it's a known wart.

What we compared, and what won

Five models and six ways of counting words: 30 combinations, each scored on the same held-back reviews.

First attempt: raw text

We started by giving unprocessed review text to five classifiers: Random Forest, Multinomial Naive Bayes, a Support Vector Classifier, XGBoost and XGBoost's random-forest mode. Random Forest led on accuracy, recall and F1, and the Support Vector Classifier led on precision.

The scores looked good, but the models failed to perform on unseen data that wasn't part of the dataset. We went back to the text, added the preprocessing from the last section, and tried n-grams.

    AUC was computed from each model's predicted labels, not its probabilities. For a two-class problem that makes it the same as balanced accuracy, so it is a useful cross-check but not a separate measure of how well the model ranks reviews.

    Every accuracy above is a multiple of 1/320, so each score comes from 320 test reviews. At that size, one review is 0.3 points, and a rough 95% margin on 88% accuracy is about ±3.6 points. So Naive Bayes, Random Forest and the Support Vector Classifier are closer than their ranking suggests.

    The gains from preprocessing were also small and uneven. Naive Bayes improved from 85.9% on raw text to 88.1%, while Random Forest finished exactly where it started at 86.25%. The best score beats the raw-text Random Forest by 1.9 points, which is inside that margin. We'd call the result a solid baseline, not proof that one model is better.

    Checking who wrote the review

    Most fake-review work looks only at the text. We also wanted to ask whether the reviewer was ever there.

    The idea

    Before a review reaches the model, the web app asks for a username, a hotel name and a booking confirmation code, and checks them against a booking record. If they don't match, the review is turned away and never classified. Only reviews from verified reservations get a verdict.

    How it works

    The checks run in order and stop at the first failure: does the username exist, does that user have a booking at this hotel, and does the confirmation code match that booking? If all three pass, the review goes through the same cleaning and vectorizing as in training and into the classifier. The saved model and vectorizer are loaded into a small Flask app, and the result is printed as one line: genuine or fake.

    The booking record is a small table of 50 made-up bookings that we wrote for the hotels in the dataset, which stands in for the data from the booking system of a real platform.

    The app itself

    The form takes four inputs, and the app answers with a verdict or one of three error messages. Pick a scenario in the replay below to see each one. This replay simulates how the app ran.

    What we'd flag

    The gate is blunt: it turns away anything it can't verify, including honest reviews from people who booked somewhere else, and it is only as good as the booking data behind it. It is also hotel-specific. For other products, the stand-in would be something like a verified purchase. The two halves were also never tested together. The classifier was scored on a research corpus of reviews, and the booking table was invented to match it, so we can't say how they would behave on real traffic.

    The paper is called A Review-and-Reviewer based approach for Fake Review Detection. We presented it at the Fourth International Conference on Electrical, Computer and Communication Technologies, and it was published in the IEEE proceedings. Check it out on IEEE Xplore: doi: 10.1109/ICECCT52121.2021.9616697

    What it says

    Most earlier work judges either the text of a review or the person who wrote it, and we tried both for our project. The paper walks through the NLP pipeline (tokenizing, lemmatizing, vectorizing), compares five classifiers on the same features, and puts the best one behind a web app that also checks the reviewer's details. Multinomial Naive Bayes on single words and pairs came out on top at 88.1% accuracy.

    What it leaves open

    The paper is upfront about two things. Asking users to type in their own details is tedious, so it suggests building that information into the model itself. And it says the approach could carry over to other services such as hospitals, restaurants, theatres and shops. While that was a part of our original project proposal, we did not get to actually test it due to time constraints.

    From project to pitch

    The same project was reused for a course on technology entrepreneurship, where we played a startup selling it.

    The exercise

    The course asked us to act as a company, with the output of our project work as our product. Over its assignments we made a brochure, a pamphlet, an advertisement video, and a website. The pitch was a service for e-commerce platforms that filters fraudulent reviews as they are posted, so shoppers never see them. What we said set it apart was that it uses several signals together, the review text and the reviewer, rather than judging text alone. Visit the startup's website, built for the same exercise, hosted on Google Sites.

    How the pitch relates to the project

    The pitch was built on our original proposal: reviews from any e-commerce site, filtered in real time. Within the time we had, we trained and tested on hotel reviews only. The model was trained on Google Colab. Only the web frontend ran on our own machines, and we didn't deploy it, because the evaluation didn't require that. The exercise kept the bigger plan in view, and made it clear which parts we had shown and which were still ahead.

    Looking back

    What didn't go as planned, and what we'd change.

    Hand-made features backfired

    Early on we added hand-made features next to the text, such as length and digit counts. The result was a collapse: Naive Bayes fell to 50.25% accuracy with 7.5% recall, which is chance. The experiment says more about how we built the features than about whether they could help. We dropped them and went back to the text alone.

    Look at the middle of the pipeline

    The regexes turn “weren't” into “weren”, and the lemmatizer, run without part-of-speech tags, turns “was” into “wa”. The model learns around them, but this could have been caught much earlier.

    One test set isn't a verdict

    Every score came from 320 test reviews, where a single review moves accuracy by 0.3 points. Naive Bayes, Random Forest and the Support Vector Classifier are within the noise of each other. Cross-validation would have given us a steadier ranking and an honest error bar.

    The data decides what a score means

    The fake reviews were written to order by paid workers, so 88% means we can tell those from real ones. Our own first models looked good on the test split and then failed on unseen data, which is the same warning from the other side.

    With more time

    We would run the original plan on other kinds of product reviews, and test the booking check and the classifier together on real traffic instead of a made-up table. We would cross-validate, fix the two preprocessing quirks, and deploy the app, so the replay on this page could be the real thing.

    Glossary

    The terms and abbreviations used on this page, in plain language.
    Accuracy
    The share of test reviews the model labelled correctly.
    AUC
    Area under the ROC curve: a score from 0.5 (guessing) to 1 (perfect) for how well a model separates two classes.
    Classifier
    A model that sorts items into classes. Ours sorts reviews into genuine and fake.
    Cross-validation
    Scoring a model several times on different splits of the data and averaging the results, for a steadier score.
    Deceptive review
    A fake review.
    F1 score
    One number that balances precision and recall.
    Held-out test reviews
    Reviews set aside before training and used only to score the model.
    Lemmatization
    Reducing a word to its dictionary form, such as “towels” to “towel”.
    Multinomial Naive Bayes
    A simple classifier that treats each word's count as independent evidence.
    N-gram
    A run of n neighbouring words: a unigram is one word, a bigram is a pair, a trigram is a triple.
    NLP
    Natural language processing: computer methods for working with human language.
    Pearson correlation
    A number from −1 to 1 for how closely two measurements rise and fall together. Zero means no linear relationship.
    Polarity
    Whether a review is positive or negative in tone.
    Precision
    Precision is the share of reviews called fake that really were fake.
    Recall
    Recall is the share of the fake reviews that the model found.
    Random Forest
    A Random Forest classifier has many decision trees vote.
    SVC
    A Support Vector Classifier (SVC) draws the widest possible boundary between the classes.
    XGBoost
    XGBoost adds trees one at a time, each correcting the last.
    Regular expression
    A pattern for finding and replacing text, such as stripping punctuation.
    Stop words
    Very common words, such as “the” and “at”, that are dropped before counting.
    TF-IDF
    Term frequency–inverse document frequency: a word's weight goes up when it appears often in one review and down when it appears in many reviews.
    Vectorizing
    Turning text into a list of numbers that a model can use.
