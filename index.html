Capstone Project

Detecting fake hotel reviews with NLP and a booking check

We trained a classifier to tell genuine hotel reviews from deceptive ones, and put a booking check in front of it so only reviews from verified reservations reach the model. It started as a capstone project in a group of three and was later published at IEEE ICECCT 2021.

Why fake reviews are worth solving

Reviews steer what people buy, and shoppers know they can't fully trust them. That gap is what we wanted to work on.

What shoppers told us

At the start of the project we ran a short survey. 74% of respondents said they were likely to decide based on reviews (4 or 5 on a 5-point scale). Yet 61% had felt some positive reviews were posted on the company's behalf, and only 4% said no. 51% had come across reviews written out of spite.

On 1-to-5 scales the picture is similar. Most people lean on reviews, 45% often see reviews that talk about the brand more than the product, and only 40% were likely to answer a "was this review useful?" prompt.

Who answered

156 people answered a Google Form that we shared with classmates and friends over five days in September 2020. Respondents came from every age band, from under 15 to over 45, though most (about 86%) were 16 to 25. Read it as a snapshot of young online shoppers, not a poll of everyone. Two people left age blank and we counted them as 16 to 25, as in our report.

What already existed

We surveyed the market for tools that do something similar. ReviewMeta (archived version) re-scored Amazon reviews and gave an adjusted rating. Fakespot (archived version) graded reviews on a handful of shopping sites. The Review Index (archived version) summarised the reviews for a product.

They shared two limits. Each worked only on specific sites, and each judged reviews after they were already posted, so nothing stopped the fake ones from going up. The market has moved since: Mozilla shut Fakespot down in July 2025, and ReviewMeta now appears to be offline.

What we tried instead

We combined two signals: what a review says, and who wrote it. A classifier reads the text, and a booking check confirms the reviewer actually has a reservation before the review is classified.

We trained and tested on hotel reviews because that's where a labelled dataset existed, and our time was limited. The original goal was reviews for any kind of e-commerce product, and the booking check is the hotel-specific stand-in for something like a verified purchase. Testing other product categories is the obvious next step.

The data we trained on

We didn't collect our own reviews. We used a public research dataset of hotel reviews where every review already carries a label.

What's in it

The corpus holds 1,600 reviews of 20 Chicago hotels, perfectly balanced: 800 truthful and 800 deceptive, half positive and half negative, with 80 reviews per hotel. Credit for collecting it is in the footer.

A caveat about the labels

The deceptive reviews weren't caught in the wild. They were written to order. That makes the labels reliable, but it narrows what a good score means: our model learns to tell these paid-for reviews from real ones, not to spot every fake on the internet. The truthful reviews are also assumed to be genuine.

What simple counts can't tell you

Before any NLP, we checked whether crude measurements would give fakes away: length, word and sentence counts, and the number of digits. Mostly they didn't. Characters, words and non-word characters barely correlate with the label (between −0.03 and −0.05) and are almost identical to each other. Polarity has no correlation at all (0.00), as expected from the balanced design.

The one real signal was digits: 66% of truthful reviews contain at least one, against 36% of deceptive ones (r = −0.31). Real guests may mention prices and room numbers, though we can't say that from the data alone. It was still nowhere near enough, which is why we moved on to the words themselves.
